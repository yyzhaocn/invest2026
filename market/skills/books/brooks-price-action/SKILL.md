## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

**The market is either trending or in a trading range.** That is true of every time frame down to an individual bar (trend bar vs. doji). Markets exhibit inertia: if it is trending, most reversal attempts fail (≈80% become flags); if it is in a range, most breakout attempts fail.

### Always-In Direction
The single most important read on any chart. At any moment, if forced to be long or short all day, what would you hold? Once you can name the always-in direction, trade with it. Setups only minimize risk — they do not create the edge. In a strong trend you can enter at the market with a small stop, anytime.

### Spike and Channel (Ch 21)
Every trend has two phases: a **spike** (urgent, one-sided, breakaway-gap-like, usually 1–10 bars) and a **channel** (two-sided, "wall of worry," overlapping bars). Almost all trends begin with a spike, so almost all trends are variations of spike and channel. Trade the spike aggressively; trade the channel as a trending range (buy below prior bar lows). Channels usually end at a measured move or a third push; the with-trend channel breakout usually fails within ~5 bars — don't chase it, wait for the pullback.

### Bar Counting: High 1/2/3, Low 1/2/3 (Introduction, Ch 1)
Count the legs of a pullback. In a bull pullback, the first bar whose high exceeds the prior bar's high is **High 1**; the next occurrence is **High 2** (an ABC correction); High 3 = wedge bull flag. Mirror for **Low 1/2/3** in bear trends. The entry is timed to the count: a High 2 with a bull signal bar at the moving average is the classic high-probability with-trend entry. A failed High 4/low 4 often signals loss of control by that side.

### Two Legs (Ch 20)
The market tries everything twice. Trends, pullbacks, and reversals subdivide into two legs. A trend line break or capitulation usually needs a two-legged test before conviction. This is Brooks' replacement for Elliott Wave: "essentially useless to the vast majority of traders."

### Trend Lines (Ch 13) and Trend Channel Lines (Ch 14)
Trend line = below lows in bull trend (buy on pullbacks). Trend channel line = above highs (fade overshoots). Lines flatten as trends age; each break = first sign of two-sided control. **Most breakouts fail** — evaluate risk AND probability, not just reward. Wedges = converging lines = reversal setups. If you keep redrawing channel lines, you're likely on the wrong side.

### Signs of Strength (Ch 19)
A ~25-point checklist for strong trends: trending swings, little body overlap, no climaxes, small sideways pullbacks, weak/ugly signal bars (strong trends keep traders out), micro gaps, urgency, countertrend setups look better than with-trend ones. The more signs present, the more you should only take with-trend entries. In the strongest trends you don't need a setup at all.

### Breakout Strength (Intro / Ch 3)
A strong breakout has: large body, small tails, volume 10–20× average, spike of 5–10 bars, micro gaps, breaks several resistance levels by many ticks, first pullback doesn't reach the breakout point. Stronger breakout → more likely follow-through and a measured move.

### Reversal Bar Minimums (Ch 5)
Bull reversal bar: close above open OR close above midpoint. Best bars: open near/below prior close, lower tail 1/3–1/2 of bar, little overlap, strong entry bar follow-through, closes above the closes/highs of several bars. In a trading range (heavy overlap) a reversal bar is meaningless — there is nothing to reverse.

### Second Entries (Ch 10)
A second entry is almost always more likely to profit than a first entry. Most good second entries are at the same price or worse — **a good fill often equals a bad trade**. In strong moves, never fade the first reversal attempt; wait for the second.

### The Close Matters (Ch 8)
The close is where institutions (who trade line charts of closes) make decisions. Don't enter before a bar closes — occasionally you gain a tick, but the tail risk is losing ~8 ticks. One strong close-based rule: enter on stops beyond the closed bar.
