# Chapter 9: Exchange-Traded Funds and Inverse Charts

## Core Idea
Changing the chart (ETFs, inverse ETFs, different time frames, bar/line charts) can make price action clearer. The SPY is nearly identical to the Emini but often easier to read; the inverse ETF (SDS) sometimes reveals a pattern you misread.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Inverse Chart Cross-Check**: If a bull flag "doesn't seem quite right," look at the inverse ETF (SDS, 2× inverse of SPY). If the pattern looks like a rounding bottom there, don't buy the Emini flag — wait for more price action (or the breakout and fade it if it fails). Use: as a second opinion on ambiguous patterns, not a primary tool.
- **ETF Discrepancy Awareness**: ETFs take fees and don't perfectly track; on triple witching days the SPY often gaps much larger than the Emini due to price adjustment. Use: don't be alarmed by the disparity — they trade tick-for-tick through the day; trade the price action.

## Key Concepts
- **SPY**: SPDR S&P 500 ETF, near-identical to Emini; smaller tick size can make patterns clearer.
- **SDS**: 2× inverse of SPY — flips the picture to check your read.
- **Triple witching**: quarterly expiration day; ETF price adjustments create larger gaps.

## Mental Models
- Think of the inverse chart as a mirror to expose misreads: if the flag doesn't look like a flag upside down, it probably isn't.
- Think of ETF discrepancies as noise — the tick-for-tick relationship is what matters.

## Anti-patterns
- **Watching too many instruments**: "it is usually not worth looking at these and it is better to stick with the Emini and sometimes the SDS."
- **Trading the gap discrepancy on triple witching**: adjusted, not meaningful.

## Key Takeaways
1. Use SPY as a clearer read of the Emini when needed.
2. Check the inverse ETF (SDS) when a pattern feels wrong — it can expose misreads.
3. Ignore ETF fee/expiration discrepancies; they trade tick-for-tick with the future.
4. Stick to one primary instrument; extras are occasional second opinions.
