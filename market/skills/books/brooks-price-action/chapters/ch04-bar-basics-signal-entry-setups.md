# Chapter 4: Bar Basics — Signal Bars, Entry Bars, Setups, and Candle Patterns

## Core Idea
A **setup** is a chart pattern suggesting a trade; the prior bar becomes a **signal bar** only after your entry fills; the fill bar is the **entry bar**. The single most important skill is judging whether there will be more buyers or sellers above/below the prior bar — signal bars in the right context mark those imbalances.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Setup → Signal Bar → Entry Bar Anatomy**: Every bar is a setup for both long and short (there are buy stops above and sell stops below every bar). A setup becomes a signal bar only when a trade is entered on the next bar. Use: keep a running set of both-direction breakout orders; the bar that triggers your order is your signal bar.
- **With-Trend vs. Countertrend**: Trades in the direction of the recent trend are with-trend; opposite are countertrend. Countertrend requires much stronger signal bars. Use: beginners should only trade with-trend, and only when the signal bar is a trend bar in the trade direction.
- **Signal Bar Quality Scaling**: The stronger the trend, the *less* important the signal bar looks — strong trends show weak/ugly signal bars on purpose (they keep traders out, forcing chasing). The more countertrend the entry, the more you need a strong signal bar. Use: don't reject a with-trend setup because the signal bar is weak in a strong trend.
- **Imbalance Principle**: You have an edge when you believe there is an imbalance of buyers/sellers above or below the prior bar. The edge is always small because equally smart traders take the other side. Use: only place orders where the price action suggests an imbalance exists.

## Key Concepts
- **Signal bar**: prior bar once your stop is filled; always labeled in hindsight.
- **Entry bar**: the bar during which you enter.
- **Follow-through bar**: the bar after entry; a second bar in your direction improves odds.
- **Candle pattern names (hammer, harami, etc.)**: irrelevant and misleading — "the power of the gods" appeal. Only trend bar vs. doji matters.
- **One-tick traps**: stocks often reverse one tick past a signal bar — for stocks, place entry stops a couple of ticks beyond.

## Mental Models
- Think of every bar as a one-bar trading range with a breakout in both directions.
- Think of candle names as noise: "each bar is important only in relation to price action."
- Think of the market as always having a smart, convinced opponent: "never be convinced of anything."

## Anti-patterns
- **Relying on candle-pattern mysticism**: names add thinking overhead and distract from the trend read.
- **Entering on every triggered stop**: a triggered stop makes it a signal bar, but not every signal bar is worth taking (avoid tight-range signals).
- **Entering before the bar closes**: occasionally saves a tick; regularly costs ~8 ticks on the trap (see Ch 8).

## Worked Example
A bull reversal bar forms three minutes into a 5-minute bar, looking great — then 5–10 seconds before close it grows 4+ ticks, its high now near the top of a bear flag. Decision: buying above its high = buying at the top of a bear flag. Until you're consistently profitable and read fast, *don't take it* — wait for a second entry. The risk is significant whenever several large range bars overlap (many trapped bulls above).

## Key Takeaways
1. A signal bar only exists after your entry fills — prepare orders, don't label in advance.
2. Stronger trend → weaker-looking signal bars are fine; countertrend → demand strong signal bars.
3. Trade with-trend as a beginner; require the signal bar to be a trend bar in your direction.
4. Avoid candle-name jargon; read trend vs. doji and the imbalance above/below the bar.
