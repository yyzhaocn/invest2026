# Chapter 6: Signal Bars — Other Types

## Core Idea
Beyond classic reversal bars, traders use strong trend bars, two-bar/three-bar reversals, small bars (inside, ii, iii, ioi), micro double tops/bottoms, and reversal-bar failures as signals. All are meaningless without price action suggesting the breakout will go far enough for at least a scalp.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Strong Trend Bar Signal**: Especially in the spike phase. After a strong breakout bar closes, buy at the market or on a stop one tick above its high; the urgency creates a series of bull bars and a growing spike. Use: enter with trend on strong trend bars in spikes; also the *first bear bar in a bull spike* is a buy (and vice versa).
- **Two-Bar Reversal**: The most common reversal setup. A strong move one direction, then a strong move the other. Classic: consecutive trend bars of similar size, opposite directions (bull = bear bar then bull bar). All climactic reversals/reversal bars are two-bar reversals on some time frame. Overlap rule: if the reversal bar overlaps >75% of the prior bar, treat the pair as a two-bar reversal (enter beyond both bars), not a reversal bar alone.
- **Three-Bar Reversal**: Two-bar reversal with an unremarkable middle bar. If the third 5-min bar coincides with the 15-min bar close, it's a 15-min reversal bar — more reliable and worth swinging more.
- **Small Bars**: Inside bar (more reliable if small with opposite-trend body), ii/iii (two/three consecutive inside bars), small bar near high/low of a big bar or range, ioi (inside-outside-inside, often breakout mode — place both stops). Large dojis are rarely good signals.
- **Micro Double Bottom/Top**: Consecutive/nearly consecutive identical lows (bottoms) or highs (tops). In a bear spike as a one-bar bear flag: consider selling one tick below identical lows; otherwise usually a reversal pattern (most small bull reversals come from micro double bottoms).

## Key Concepts
- **Breakout mode**: ioi patterns where the move can go either way; place a sell stop below and a buy stop above, enter in the breakout direction.
- **Reversal bar failure**: pattern fails to yield a scalp, reverses — traps traders, fueling the opposite move.
- **Weak signal bars in strong trends**: a pullback setup that "looks perfect and easy" usually means the trend is NOT strong — it becomes the final flag.

## Mental Models
- Think of every reversal as a two-bar reversal on the right time frame — you don't need to find the perfect chart.
- Think "the trend requires traders to keep missing entries" — weak signals keep beginners out.
- Think of a strong trend bar as a gap/spike/climax/breakout all at once; context picks the trade.

## Anti-patterns
- **Taking every triggered signal**: many signals in tight trading ranges are best avoided.
- **Entering on large-doji signals**: one-bar trading ranges — wait for second signals.
- **Trusting perfect-looking pullback setups**: they often mark the final flag of a trend.

## Worked Example
A strong bull trend has a two-legged pullback to the moving average. Traders buy above the prior bar's high even though it's a bear trend bar — they fear the pullback will reverse and the rally accelerate; the urgent buying produces a fast rally to new highs. Traders who skipped the weak-looking signal keep buying in small pieces up the whole way. Trapped shorts keep waiting for a deeper correction that never comes. Most trends only get a real correction after a minor climax when the last weak traders finally enter late and the last wrong-side traders exit.

## Key Takeaways
1. Strong trend bars in spikes are with-trend signals — buy closes or above highs.
2. Two-bar reversals are everywhere; use the >75% overlap rule to choose entry beyond both bars.
3. ioi = breakout mode: place both stops, enter the breakout.
4. In strong trends, weak-looking signal bars are normal — don't require perfection for with-trend entries.
