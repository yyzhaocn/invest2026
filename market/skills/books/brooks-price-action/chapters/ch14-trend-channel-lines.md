# Chapter 14: Trend Channel Lines

## Core Idea
The trend channel line is on the opposite side of price action from the trend line, with the same general slope. Its purpose: fading a trend that has gone too far, too fast — especially on a second penetration/overshoot that reverses.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Overshoot and Reverse**: Look for a trend channel line overshoot that reverses, especially a *second* penetration. Most reversals occur after a channel line overshoot — it's the entrenched institutional read that price went too far. Use: fade the overshoot only with a strong reversal bar (or wait for the pullback from a trend line break).
- **Wedge = Converging Channel**: When trend line and channel line converge in a rising/falling channel, it's a wedge — a reversal setup. Most wedges have a failed trend channel line breakout as the reversal trigger; most channel overshoot reversals are also wedge reversals (even without a perfect wedge shape). Use: treat overshoots and wedges as the same trade.
- **Channel as Flag**: Any channel sloping up is a bear flag (downside breakout likely); any channel sloping down is a bull flag. The with-trend breakout usually fails within ~5 bars. Use: don't chase the breakout; expect the pullback or fade.
- **Redrawing Warning**: If you find yourself repeatedly redrawing trend channel lines, you're likely on the wrong side — looking for a reversal in a trend that keeps strengthening. Use: trade with the trend until it clearly reverses; "never trade against the trend until it has clearly reversed."

## Key Concepts
- **Trend channel line**: parallel of the trend line dragged to the opposite side, or drawn across swing points on that side.
- **Overshoot**: price penetrating the channel line, usually reversing.
- **Final flag slope**: the slope of the final flag of a trend often approximates the slope of the new trend (minor observation).
- **Vacuum/parabolic climax logic**: last shorts covering + naive bulls buying = sharp spike above the line, then no buyers left → reversal. (Whether the story is literally true is irrelevant — the pattern is real.)

## Mental Models
- Think of the channel line as "the point of maximum wrongness" for the trend — the fade.
- Think of wedges and overshoots as the same reversal setup wearing different clothes.
- Think "if I keep redrawing lines, I'm fighting the trend — stop."

## Anti-patterns
- **Redrawing channel lines repeatedly** (on the wrong side).
- **Entering countertrend without a strong reversal bar at the overshoot** or a pullback from a strong trend line break.
- **Trading against a trend "because it looks overdue"**: regression-to-the-mean thinking loses accounts.

## Worked Example
In a bull channel, the market works up to a resistance level, pokes through it, the last shorts cover in unison, and euphoric bulls buy the spike above the trend channel line. The spike up causes more shorts to cover and more naive longs to enter — self-feeding. Then there are no shorts left to cover and the trapped longs panic when the move stalls: one-sided selling. Smart traders fade the overshoot with a strong reversal bar, or wait for a pullback from the trend line break before shorting.

## Key Takeaways
1. Fade trend channel line overshoots with strong reversal bars — especially the second penetration.
2. Wedges (converging channels) = reversal setups; treat overshoots and wedges as one trade.
3. Rising channel = bear flag; falling channel = bull flag; with-trend breakouts usually fail within ~5 bars.
4. Keep redrawing lines? You're on the wrong side — trade with the trend until it clearly reverses.
